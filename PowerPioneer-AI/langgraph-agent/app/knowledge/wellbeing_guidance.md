# ShiftGuard wellbeing guidance

Prototype content for the ShiftGuard demo. Replace these sections with your organisation's approved policies;
`search_wellbeing_guidance` reads every `## ` section of this file. Keep one topic per section.

## Rest between shifts
Aim for at least 11 consecutive hours of rest between the end of one shift and the start of the next (the UK
Working Time Regulations daily rest minimum). Avoid "quick returns" such as a late shift followed by an early shift.
When cover is needed, prefer colleagues who will still get 11 hours of rest either side of the extra shift.

## Rest breaks during a shift
Anyone working more than 6 hours is entitled to an uninterrupted break of at least 20 minutes, away from their
workstation. Repeatedly missed breaks are an early sign of overload: if someone has missed several breaks in a month,
look at staffing on those shifts first, then agree a protected break time with them.

## Night shifts and consecutive nights
Limit runs of night shifts to 4 in a row where possible and allow at least 46 hours off after a run of nights.
Rotate forward (day, then late, then night) rather than backwards. Offer a check-in to anyone who has worked many
night shifts in the last month, especially if they also report poor sleep.

## Overtime and workload
Overtime should be voluntary and spread fairly. If someone's hours are well above their contract for several weeks,
discuss it with them, review the rota and avoid offering them further extra shifts until hours come back down.
Understaffed shifts and high patient load raise risk for the whole team: escalate persistent gaps to the
staffing lead.

## Supportive 1:1 check-ins
A wellbeing 1:1 is a conversation, not a performance review. Hold it somewhere private, start with open questions
("How have the last few weeks been?"), listen more than you talk, agree one or two practical changes and a date to
follow up. Never mention risk scores or models to the employee; talk about the workload and the support available.

## End-of-life care and patient death
Caring for a dying patient is emotionally demanding even for experienced staff. Offer a short debrief after an
end-of-life care shift or a patient death, make sure the person can take their break, and avoid rostering several
end-of-life care shifts back to back for the same person. Remind them of the employee assistance programme and
chaplaincy or bereavement support.

## Employee assistance programme
Employees can use the confidential employee assistance programme (EAP) at any time, free of charge, for emotional,
financial or legal support. Managers can share the details but cannot see who uses it. Mention it in every
supportive conversation.

## Return to work after maternity leave
Agree a phased return plan before the first day back: predictable shifts, no unplanned overtime in the first weeks,
protected breaks for breastfeeding or recovery, and a private space to express milk. Check in weekly for the first
12 weeks and honour agreed adjustments; repeated short-notice changes undermine the return.

## Annual leave and proper breaks
A proper break of 5 or more consecutive days off helps people recover. If someone has not had one for several
months, encourage them to book leave. Think carefully before rejecting a leave request from someone under strain;
if it must be declined, offer alternative dates straight away.

## Short-notice roster changes
Give as much notice as possible of rota changes. Frequent short-notice changes make it hard to rest and plan family
life. When a change within 72 hours is unavoidable, ask rather than tell, and thank the colleague who helps out.

## Buddy and peer support
A buddy is a colleague on similar shifts who checks in informally. Good buddies share shifts, have capacity
(supporting no more than 3 people) and are not under heavy strain themselves. Buddies never receive risk scores,
only a prompt to check in.

## Sickness absence and returning after illness
After an unplanned absence, hold a short, supportive return-to-work conversation. Ask whether work contributed and
whether any temporary adjustments would help. Several short absences in a few months can point to fatigue or
stress: offer support before considering any formal process.
